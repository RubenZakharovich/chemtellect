Chemtellect: findings from Notebook 07

A visual findings companion for “Efficiency Evaluation of HNSW-based Approximate Molecular Retrieval”. All measured values below come from the saved outputs of 07_dataset_pipeline.ipynb, chemicalspace_new/scale_1m_00, experiment_20261112_0333. These are the new nested 1K–1M results; they are not pooled with Notebook 06’s separate acquisition. Derived ratios use the displayed numeric tables. No new benchmarks were run.

1. What do the measurements mean?

The measured trade-off is speed versus recall.

Headline latency is the median across 100 queries of each query’s median of five measured times. Lower is better. Mean top-k recall is the average tie-aware recovery of the best k neighbors over those 100 queries. Higher is better. Recall uses the first measured pass; five timing repetitions are not five independent quality experiments.

Lowest observed recall is min[R_k(q)] over the 100 evaluated queries for one size and k. It is the performance of the worst observed query under this metric, not the fraction of queries that performed badly, not the lowest similarity score, and not a guaranteed floor for future queries. At 1M and k=10, 20% means one query received credit for only 2 of the best 10 neighbors. At k=50, 12% means 6 of 50. The minimum at each k need not belong to the same query; the displayed summaries cannot establish that.

5. How much missing coverage is caused by k, and how much by HNSW?

For each nonempty ground-truth set, exact top-k retrieval reaches min(1, k / |Gτ(q)|). Therefore the exact threshold mean is the mean capacity ceiling. The chart decomposes 100% as: HNSW mean coverage + (exact mean − HNSW mean) + (100% − exact mean). Subtraction is valid because both methods use the same defined queries within each panel.

6. Does requesting more neighbors help?

At 1M, increasing k from 10 to 100 raises τ=0.3 HNSW threshold recall from 5.76% to 31.40%, and τ=0.5 recall from 83.07% to 92.51%. Headline latency rises from 1.719 to 1.804 ms, about 4.9%. Over the same change, mean top-k recall declines from 97.40% to 96.66%. There is no contradiction: top-100 asks for a larger and harder exact membership set, while a larger returned set can cover more threshold matches.

The k=25 mean top-k recall is slightly higher than k=10, and k=100 latency is slightly lower than k=50. Do not describe these small nonmonotonic differences as an optimized k or a general speed law. No independent-repeat uncertainty interval was estimated. Increasing k also changes how many results a user must review; there is no universal best k.

The 1M HNSW graph takes about 1,986 seconds (33.10 minutes) to construct. Fingerprint preparation for the full selected artifact has a recorded stage wall time of 236.35 seconds. These costs are outside per-query latency and are paid before repeated searches can benefit from the graph.

The notebook discloses an encoding optimization: 1K–100K used NumPy scalar-to-string conversion, while 1M used a byte lookup producing identical strings. This explains why conversion time can decrease at the largest size. Do not interpret that preparation column as pure dataset-size scaling. The saved report states that native graph construction, prepared queries and scientific search settings were unchanged.

For an illustrative cost calculation at k=10, dividing the 1M graph build alone (1,986.29 seconds) by the headline FPSim2-minus-HNSW saving (0.035727 seconds) gives about 55,596 searches. This is a graph-build-only amortization estimate using representative query times, not a measured deployment break-even; it excludes differences in other preparation costs, load/save, updates and request distributions.

The 1M worker’s process RSS snapshots rise from 154 MiB before loading to 3,603 MiB after validation and comparative passes, with 3,239 MiB just after the graph is built. Multiple representations coexist. These are process snapshots, not isolated HNSW memory, peak memory, or a basis for declaring which backend uses the least RAM. The fresh 100×1M float64 reference matrix alone represents 800 MB of score values in decimal units, excluding headers and metadata.

9. Paper-ready findings text

On a single sampled chemicalspace_new parent collection, the benchmark compared RDKit, FPSim2 and HNSW over nested indexes of 1,000, 10,000, 100,000 and 1,000,000 molecules using 100 fixed held-out queries. FPSim2 had the lowest headline latency at the two smaller sizes, whereas HNSW was fastest at 100,000 and 1,000,000 records. At one million records, HNSW latency ranged from 1.719 to 1.809 ms across the tested candidate counts, compared with 37.447–38.262 ms for FPSim2 and 133.129–136.477 ms for RDKit. Relative to FPSim2, this corresponded to approximately 21.1–21.8-fold speedup.

The speed advantage was accompanied by incomplete nearest-neighbor recovery. Mean HNSW tie-aware top-k recall ranged from 96.66% to 97.56% at one million records, while both exact methods achieved 100% in the evaluated responses. The lowest observed HNSW recall ranged from 12% to 20%, showing that strong average recovery did not ensure reliable performance for every query. These minima describe the evaluated sample and are not guarantees about future queries.

Threshold recall distinguished missed nearest neighbors from limitations imposed by the result budget. At one million records and k=100, HNSW mean recall at τ=0.3 was 31.40%, compared with an exact top-100 ceiling of 31.63%. Most uncovered qualifying records were therefore beyond the capacity of a 100-result response. At τ=0.5, HNSW recovered 92.51%, compared with the 96.25% exact ceiling, indicating an additional approximation shortfall. At τ=0.7, HNSW recovered all qualifying records, but only 10 of the 100 queries had nonempty ground truth; no query had qualifying records at τ=0.9. Consequently, higher threshold means should be interpreted together with their denominators and capacity ceilings.

The findings support HNSW as a faster retrieval option at the larger tested sizes when some membership loss is acceptable. This benefit requires an upfront graph construction investment of approximately 33.10 minutes at one million records and further investigation of difficult queries. The experiment used one acquisition, one graph per size and one fixed scientific configuration; it does not establish parameter-optimal performance, independent-build variability, isolated peak memory, biological relevance or performance at larger unmeasured scales.

10. Stronger follow-up views and precise paper edits

For the current paper, use Figures 1–3 as the core sequence: when approximation becomes faster; what average and worst-observed quality are exchanged; and why low threshold coverage need not mean poor graph search. Figure 4 is a practical candidate-budget companion. Keep the complete numeric tables in an appendix if space is tight.

With the saved per-query files, add an empirical cumulative distribution of top-k recall, the fraction of queries below chosen recall cutoffs, and a per-query scatter of top-k versus threshold recall faceted by τ. Pair difficult-query identity, exact qualifying counts and query descriptors to investigate failures. Those views cannot be reconstructed faithfully from means and minima alone. A true latency–quality tuning frontier would require an efSearch sweep with fixed queries, k, fingerprints and graph; multiple independent graph builds would then measure construction variability.

Repair the incomplete “High top-k / Low threshold” row in the paper to read: “HNSW recovers most nearest neighbors, but the returned set may cover only a small fraction of all threshold-qualified records; compare against the exact top-k capacity ceiling.” Treat the four high/low combinations as conceptual per-query possibilities, not four observed groups demonstrated by aggregate means. The current summaries do not establish actual counts in those groups.

Separate the proposed parameter grid from the executed fixed configuration. Update the tested-size list to include 1M and identify Notebook 07’s parent sample explicitly. Keep historical Notebook 06 results labeled as a separate experiment. Avoid calling recall precision, avoid reporting τ=0.9 as perfect recall, and avoid claiming all 100 queries support the τ=0.7 result.
